## intro

The Inter-High School Championships (全国高等学校総合体育大会, commonly "Inter-High") is the summer national football tournament for Japanese high schools. It is a straight knockout: one champion school per prefecture, with an extra place for Tokyo, Kanagawa, Osaka and the host prefecture, played over eight days in the hottest weeks of the year. Matches last 70 minutes, shorter than the 90 minutes of the Premier League, and are decided by penalties if level. J.League club academies do not take part — this competition is for school teams only, which makes it, with the winter All Japan High School Soccer Tournament, one of the two titles a Japanese high school can win.

## result

**Shizuoka Gakuen won the 2026 title, beating Kindai University High School 2-1 in the final at J-Village Stadium on 1 August.** The winning goal came in the sixth minute of added time. It is Shizuoka Gakuen's first Inter-High title, fifteen years after they last reached the final, and the first time a school from Shizuoka has won the tournament since 1996. Kindai University High School, back at the tournament for the first time in twelve editions, won all five of their earlier matches by a single goal or on penalties.

## origins

Where do the players come from? Using the JFA's official registration lists, we counted the previous club of all 1,428 players from the 51 schools at the 2026 tournament.

- **1,039 players (72.8%) came from independent local clubs** — the "town clubs" that run outside both the school system and the professional academies. 210 (14.7%) came from J.League academy U-15 teams, 167 (11.7%) from junior-high school clubs and 12 from JFA Academy Fukushima.
- Those players came from **580 different clubs, and 298 of them sent exactly one player**. A national tournament squad list in Japan is not drawn from a handful of elite academies.
- The largest single sources were **FC LAVIDA and Oita Junior High School, with 20 players each**. Counted by professional club organisation, Kashima Antlers supplied the most (19).
- The highest share of academy-trained players was at **Ichiritsu Funabashi High School (53.6%)**, followed by Imabari Higashi Secondary School (39.3%) and Shoshi High School (35.7%). Three schools had none at all.
- Playing time was almost identical across the three routes: town-club players made up 72.8% of the squads and 71.7% of the minutes played, academy players 14.7% and 15.7%.

This is the opposite of the picture many people outside Japan expect. For a foreign scout, it means the pathway into a leading high school runs through hundreds of small local clubs, not through a short list of academies.
