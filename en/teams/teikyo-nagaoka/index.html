Teikyo Nagaoka High School

Teikyo Nagaoka High School — 帝京長岡 in Japanese — plays the most distinctive football of any school team in Japan. It is in Nagaoka, in Niigata, one of the snowiest inhabited places on earth, where the outdoor season is short. Rather than accept that as a limit, the club moved indoors and taught itself futsal, and then applied futsal to eleven-a-side. The result is a team from a provincial city that reached the semi-finals of the winter championship in two consecutive years and builds from the back against J.League academies. It plays in the Premier League EAST.

Niigata · founded 1981 · head coach FURUSAWA Toru

2026 season

Currently 7th in the Premier League EAST with 21 points from 14 matches (7W 0D 7L, 33-27). Updated daily.

Playing style

The coaching philosophy is stated as "let's learn football", and it is meant literally: the players are expected to read situations and find the answer, not to be told it. The manager, FURUSAWA Toru, works as a facilitator rather than a commander — still unusual in Japanese school sport, where the instruction-and-obedience model runs deep.

On the pitch it shows as possession with an unusual vocabulary. Group patterns taken straight from futsal — the rotation known as the eight, playing into a pivot, the short movement off the wing — are used deliberately to open the space in front of the opposition box. Very few teams anywhere play this way, and it comes from where the training happens.

The futsal titles are not a side project. They are the evidence for the method: the same technical base, tested in the format it came from.

History

  • 1981 — The school's football club is founded. Winter closes the pitches for months every year.
  • The gymnasium years — Training moves indoors. The floor is fast and true, so a poor first touch is punished immediately; players are forced into sharper control and quicker decisions in tight space. What starts as a compromise becomes the identity.
  • The club structure — TANIGUCHI Tetsuro, now director of football, sets up Nagaoka JYFC as a non-profit junior-youth club, and with it a pathway that starts at 12 rather than 15.
  • 2016 — A first national futsal title.
  • 2018 — A second futsal title, won 5-1 in the final.
  • 2019 and 2020 — Back-to-back semi-finals at the winter championship, with a style Japanese reporters described as closer to a professional team's than a school's. That generation included KOZUKA Kazuki, YACHIDA Teppei and TANAKA Katsuyuki.
  • 2026 — In the Premier League EAST, playing possession football against J.League academies week after week.

Development pathway

Four things hold it together.

  • Futsal applied to eleven-a-side. The indoor work builds sole-of-the-foot control, one-touch passing in confined space and the particular transition behaviour futsal demands. Against a high-pressing opponent the team does not go long; it takes the pressure apart with patterns learned on a gymnasium floor.
  • A town club rather than a feeder. Nagaoka JYFC is a community institution as much as a supply line. Players arrive at 15 with the tactical groundwork already done, which is why first-years appear in the senior side.
  • Learning rather than obedience. The programme is explicit that it rejects the command-and-shout model. What it wants is a player who can read a situation and reason towards the best answer without waiting.
  • A national recruiting net, and an open door. The core is local, but players come from junior teams in Gunma, Aichi, Shizuoka, Kanagawa and Hyogo. Players with roots outside Japan are part of the squad as a matter of course.

Former players

  • KOZUKA Kazuki (midfielder, Shimizu S-Pulse) — a passer with an unusually long career: Albirex Niigata, four other J.League clubs, two seasons in South Korea, and back.
  • YACHIDA Teppei (midfielder, Bali United, Indonesia) — from Nagaoka himself, and described at the school as the best argument for its six-year pathway.
  • TANAKA Katsuyuki (midfielder, Hokkaido Consadole Sapporo) — a left foot that changes the width of the pitch; reached the J.League through university.
  • INOKOSHI Yui (goalkeeper, Shimizu S-Pulse) — a build-from-the-back goalkeeper, which is exactly what this programme would be expected to produce.
  • HARUYAMA Misaki (forward) — a career that went from the J.League to the German lower leagues and back to a community club in Japan.

Players to watch in 2026

Players worth knowing in the 2026 season:

  • KOYAMA Masaki (forward, second year, no. 9) — six goals in the 2026 Premier League EAST, fourth in the division's scoring list, and more than a third of everything his team has scored.
  • WAJIKI Hinata (midfielder, third year, no. 14) — the captain, named in the 2026 Japan High School Select squad. He broke into the side as a first-year at the Inter-High.
  • OKANAKA Shun (forward, third year, no. 11) and NAKASHIMA Ren (forward, third year, no. 18) — the rest of the front line.
  • KOZAI Shuga (midfielder, third year, no. 10) and KIRIU Kai (midfielder, third year, no. 8) — the pair who set the tempo.
  • HOTTA Hiroa (defender, third year, no. 4) — a centre-back in a side that asks its defenders to play.
  • RAI Owen (forward, second year, no. 20) — one of several players in the squad with roots outside Japan.
  • OKAWA Reita (goalkeeper, no. 31) — came from Nagoya Grampus's under-15 team, a long way from home at 15.

Local products from Nagaoka JYFC, players recruited from academies in other prefectures, and players with international backgrounds compete for the same places, which is the balance the club is trying to hold.
