How Youth Football Works in Japan: High Schools, J.League Academies and the Road to the Pros

Japan develops footballers in a way that has no close equivalent in Europe or South America. Age groups follow the school year, the strongest youth teams are often ordinary high-school clubs with more than a hundred players, school teams and professional academies compete in the same national league, a high-school knockout tournament fills the national stadium, and for many players the step between youth football and the pros is university. This guide explains each of these differences, with the numbers behind them, for scouts, agents and fans outside Japan.

The short version

  1. Age groups follow the school year (April to March), and players change teams at 12, 15 and 18 as they move between school stages.
  2. There is more than one pathway. At every stage a young player chooses between school football and a club, most importantly between a high-school club and a J.League academy at 15.
  3. High-school clubs are huge. Leading programs have 100–280 players and enter reserve teams in lower leagues, so almost everyone plays competitive matches.
  4. Schools and academies play in one league. Since 2011 the national U-18 Premier League and the pyramid below it have mixed both kinds of team.
  5. The winter High School Tournament is a national event. The 2026 final drew 60,142 spectators, a record for the competition.
  6. University is a main road to the pros. Japan has had few reserve or U-21 teams, so many players spend 18–22 in university football. Seven of Japan's 26 players at the 2026 World Cup went that way.

1. Age groups follow the school year

In most football countries youth teams are grouped by calendar year of birth. In Japan the unit is the school year, which runs from April to March. Children born between 2 April and 1 April of the following year are in the same school year, and they move up together: elementary school (ages 6–12), junior high school (12–15) and high school (15–18).

Football registration follows the same stages. The Japan Football Association (JFA) registers players in four categories: Category 4 (elementary school age), Category 3 (junior high school age, U-15), Category 2 (high school age, U-18) and Category 1 (adults). In the 2025 season the JFA registered 148,967 players in Category 2, the U-18 age group, across 3,781 teams.

Two practical consequences for anyone reading Japanese squad lists:

  • Players are described by school year — 1年 (1st year), 2年 (2nd year), 3年 (3rd year) — not by age. A 3rd-year high-school player in the 2026 season was born between 2 April 2008 and 1 April 2009 and is in his final year of youth football.
  • Because every change of school is also a change of team, the natural transfer points are at 12, 15 and 18, and these are when most decisions about a player's future are made.

2. Several pathways, chosen again at every stage

A European academy player can stay at one club from the age of eight into his twenties. A Japanese player usually makes a new choice at each school stage.

  • Up to 12: local grassroots clubs and, for a few, J.League club academies.
  • 12–15 (U-15): the junior-high-school football club, a J.League academy U-15 team, or an independent "town club" (街クラブ). Independent U-15 clubs are an important and often overlooked source of talent.
  • 15–18 (U-18): a high-school football club or a J.League academy U-18 team. This is the big decision.

Moving from a J.League U-15 team to the same club's U-18 team is not automatic. Players who are not promoted — and some who are offered a place but prefer the school environment — go to high schools, and many strong high-school teams are built partly on former academy players. The reverse also happens at 18: academy players not offered a professional contract often continue at university.

3. Inside a Japanese high-school football club

High-school football (高校サッカー) is organised through the schools' sports federation and is part of school life, but at the top level it is closer to a full-time development program than to school sport as it is known in Europe.

Size

Squads of more than a hundred players are normal at leading schools. Examples from the team profiles on this site, each checked against the school or club's own figures: Fujieda Meisei (Shizuoka) 279 players, Yamagata Meisei (Yamagata) 184, Mie (Mie) 151 and Ryutsu Keizai University Kashiwa (Chiba) 131.

Reserve teams in real leagues

To give all those players competitive football, big schools enter second and third teams in lower divisions of the same league pyramid. For example, Ryutsu Keizai University Kashiwa plays in the national Premier League, its second team in the Prince League Kanto Division 1 and its third team in the Chiba prefectural first division. Fujieda Meisei runs seven categories, from its Prince League team down to a league for first-year players. In the Prince Leagues and prefectural top divisions that this site tracks there are more than 100 such second and third teams. Rules keep the levels apart: for example, the second team of a Premier League club cannot enter the play-off for promotion to the Premier League.

Coaches, dormitories and recruitment

Many high-school coaches are teachers at the school, while leading programs also employ full coaching staffs with professional licences. Many private schools have dormitories and attract players from all over Japan, so a "Tokyo" or "Aomori" team is not necessarily made up of local players. Training, school lessons and team life are closely linked, and the clubs place strong emphasis on discipline and education alongside football.

4. J.League club academies

Every J.League club runs an academy, and its U-18 team (named "U-18" or "Youth", for example Kashima Antlers Youth or Vissel Kobe U-18) is the direct route into its first team. Academy players attend a separate school and train with the club after classes. Squads are much smaller than those of the big high schools.

Two registration rules allow academy players to play for the professional first team while still in youth football:

  • Type-2 registration (2種登録) — an academy player registered in Category 2 can be registered to play in J.League official matches for his club's first team while remaining an academy player.
  • Professional contracts during the U-18 years — clubs increasingly sign their best academy players to professional contracts while they are still at school. When this site checked all 42 players announced as joining J.League clubs for 2027 against the clubs' official announcements, 27 of them (all from club academies) had already signed professional contracts, and 23 of those were type-2 registered. The other 15 were "provisional signings" (内定) who join after graduating.

5. One league for schools and academies

Until 2010 the main national competition for this age group was a cup. In 2011 it became a league: the Prince Takamado Trophy JFA U-18 Football Premier League, in which high-school clubs and J.League academies play each other home and away.

  • Premier League — 24 teams in EAST and WEST divisions of 12, April to December. The two division winners meet in the Premier League Final in December; the bottom two in each division are relegated.
  • Prince Leagues — 13 leagues in 9 regions. In December, 16 Prince League teams play off for 4 places in the Premier League.
  • Prefectural leagues — each of the 47 prefectures has its own divisions, feeding into the Prince Leagues through regional play-offs.

The mix is roughly even at the top. In the 2026 Premier League, 9 of the 24 teams are high-school clubs and 15 are J.League academies. See the current standings in English.

6. The tournaments — and why the winter one matters so much

Alongside the league, the season has several national knockout competitions. They are split by type of team, which is itself unusual.

All Japan High School Soccer Tournament (winter, high schools only)

First held in January 1918 (under its original name), this is the best-known youth competition in Japan. Each prefecture sends its champion (Tokyo sends two), making 48 teams, and the knockout rounds run from late December into January. The matches are broadcast nationwide, and the final is played at the Japan National Stadium in Tokyo. The 104th final, on 12 January 2026, was watched by 60,142 spectators — the largest crowd in the competition's history — as Kamimura Gakuen won the title for the first time. The 105th edition runs from 28 December 2026 to 11 January 2027.

For players this tournament can matter as much as the league: a good run brings national television exposure and media coverage that club academy players, who cannot enter it, do not get. For scouts it is also the moment when many high-school 3rd-year players are seen by the widest audience before they move on.

Inter-High School Championships (summer, high schools only)

The summer national tournament for high-school clubs, played in a compressed schedule in the hottest weeks of the year. Shizuoka Gakuen won the 2026 edition.

Japan Club Youth (U-18) Championship (club teams only)

The national championship for club academies. From 2026 it moves from summer to winter (23–29 December 2026, in Hiroshima and Yamaguchi) with 32 teams in a straight knockout, largely because of the danger of extreme summer heat to players.

J Youth Cup

A cup competition for J.League club academies.

7. After 18: the gap, and why university football matters

In Europe the years from 18 to 21 are covered by reserve teams, second teams in lower professional divisions and U-21 or U-23 leagues. Japan has had very little of this. From 2016 to 2020 a few clubs (FC Tokyo, Gamba Osaka and Cerezo Osaka) entered U-23 teams in the third division, J3, but the experiment ended. Only in the 2026/27 season has the J.League started a U-21 J.League, with 11 clubs in East and West groups.

University football filled the gap. University teams play in nine regional leagues and two national tournaments (the Prime Minister Cup in summer and the All Japan University Championship in winter), and the standard is high. The numbers show how important this route is:

  • Universities in the Kanto region alone sent 85 graduates to J.League clubs or overseas clubs for the 2026 season.
  • Seven of the 26 players in Japan's squad for the 2026 World Cup played university football, including Yuto Nagatomo (Meiji University), Junya Ito (Kanagawa University) and Ayase Ueda (Hosei University). All seven had played high-school football, not in a club academy, before university.
  • Some players now go abroad directly from university: Kento Shiogai joined NEC Nijmegen in the Netherlands in 2024 while studying at Keio University.

The Special Designated Player system (特別指定選手) connects the two worlds. It allows a university, high-school or club youth player to play in J.League official matches for a club while staying registered with his own team; a J1 or J2 club can normally take up to three such players. One of the conditions is that the player intends to sign a professional contract with the club that takes him on.

For scouts this means that the best Japanese players at 18 are not all in professional environments. A significant share of future internationals are, at 19 or 20, in university teams.

8. The calendar does not match Europe's — or even the J.League's

Youth leagues follow the school year: the Premier League runs from April to December, and players graduate in March. The J.League, however, moved to an August-to-June season from 2026/27 (the first season runs from August 2026 to June 2027).

As a result, clubs describe the arrival of their new signings in different ways — some players join in January or February 2027, halfway through the professional season, and some academy players have already joined the first-team squad. When you see a Japanese player listed as a "2027 signing", check the club's own announcement for the actual date.

9. Practical notes for scouts and agents

  • Reading team names. 高校 (kōkō) means high school; ユース ("Youth") or U-18 after a club name means a J.League academy. A name ending in 2nd or 3rd, or a letter such as B, is a reserve team.
  • Check the year. Squad lists from tournaments held in March, before the new school year, often still include players who are about to graduate. Always check which school year a list belongs to.
  • Use official sources. Club announcements and the JFA's match pages are the reliable record. Secondary summary sites often confuse a university player's former high school with his current team.
  • Watch the key moments. The Premier League season (April–December), the winter High School Tournament (late December–January) and the December Premier League Final and play-offs show the most players at the highest level.

Frequently asked questions

Do the best young players in Japan play for high schools or for J.League academies?

Both. Many future professionals come through club academies, but high-school clubs remain a major source of talent. In the 2026 Premier League, 9 of the 24 teams are high schools, and all seven university graduates in Japan's 2026 World Cup squad had played high-school football.

Why do so many Japanese players go to university before turning professional?

Japanese clubs have traditionally had few reserve teams or U-21/U-23 leagues, so university football became the main place for players aged 18 to 22 to develop. A U-21 J.League started only in the 2026/27 season.

What does "3rd year" mean in a Japanese squad list?

It is the player's school year. In high school, 3rd-year players are 17 or 18 and in their final season of youth football; the school year starts in April.

Can J.League academy players play in the High School Tournament?

No. The High School Tournament and the Inter-High are for high-school clubs only. Academy teams have their own championship, the Japan Club Youth (U-18) Championship. Both types of team meet in the Premier League and the leagues below it.
